\begin{abstract}
This report studies agents for the Pok\'emon Trading Card Game, a stochastic, imperfect-information game with variable action sets (Kaggle \emph{PTCG AI Battle Challenge}). A first generation combined a relational transformer with imitation, offline RL, self-play PPO and weight ensembling: 63\% expert-move accuracy, but at most 650 on the ladder. A second generation uses a compact transformer as prior and value for determinized Monte Carlo tree search on the exact simulator, trained with self-play and expert iteration and evaluated with a fixed gauntlet, confidence intervals and sequential tests. Search raises the gauntlet score from 0.75 to 0.90 and the ladder rating $\mu$ to $\approx$770. A learned belief over the opponent's hand predicts hidden cards better than counting yet does not improve play: one deep tree beats several shallow determinized ones.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
In the Pok\'emon TCG two players pilot 60-card decks; hands, deck order and prize cards are hidden, draws and coin flips are random, and the legal move set changes at every decision (play, attach, evolve, attack, choose targets, choose $k$ of $n$ cards). Kaggle provides 5k high-rated replays, a C++ simulator that can step a game forward once the hidden cards are guessed, and a rated ladder; agents get 2 CPUs and 600\,s of thinking per game.

Two generations of agents are presented: the first (\S\ref{sec:gen1}) asks how far learning alone goes; the second (\S\ref{sec:gen2}), motivated by its failure modes, asks how much a learned model gains from planning with the real engine. Contributions: an account of what transferred from offline metrics to play, a search agent that lifts the rating from 650 to $\approx$770, controlled ablations (belief, determinization, budget, expert iteration) and an evaluation protocol that, unlike the first one, agreed with the ladder where tested. Code: \url{https://github.com/AntoCordeiro/ptcg-agent}.

\paragraph*{Related work.} AlphaGo/AlphaZero combine policy/value networks with PUCT search \cite{silver2016mastering,silver2018general}, and expert iteration distils search into the network \cite{anthony2017thinking}. For imperfect information, perfect-information Monte Carlo samples \emph{determinizations} of the hidden state \cite{long2010understanding}; information-set MCTS shares one tree across samples \cite{cowling2012information}. The work also builds on transformers with pointer outputs \cite{vaswani2017attention,vinyals2019grandmaster}, IQL \cite{kostrikov2022offline}, PPO \cite{schulman2017proximal} and WiSE-FT \cite{wortsman2022robust}.

\section{First generation: learning without planning}\label{sec:gen1}
\paragraph*{Model.} \emph{PTCGDualCrossNet} encodes 60 state and 80 option tokens (332-d hand-crafted features with frozen card-text embeddings) and a global vector with a card-tracker belief. A 4-layer encoder ($d{=}256$) is followed by 3 cross-attention layers where options attend to the state with biases from 8 symbolic relations (e.g.\ ``evolves from''), a gated self-attention over options and an autoregressive pointer for $k$-of-$n$ choices.

\paragraph*{Training.} Behavioural cloning (BC) on 75k decisions from the top $\approx$650 replays; IQL (expectile $\tau{=}0.75$, advantage-weighted extraction); league PPO against past snapshots over 5--15 decks; WiSE-FT interpolation of BC, IQL and PPO weights. A light search vetoed deck-outs, took lethal attacks and re-ranked close ties (6\,ms/move).

\paragraph*{Findings.} Fixing a one-step observation/action misalignment in the replays raised BC accuracy from 40.9\% to 63.3\%; IQL reached 63.4\%. The extra machinery did not transfer to play: an agent rated +240 Elo \emph{better} locally (40 games) scored 500 on the ladder vs 650 for its predecessor; with 350 games the 650-agent scores only 0.32 locally. Small head-to-head tests between agents of the same project did not predict the ladder.

\section{Second generation: planning with the simulator}\label{sec:gen2}
\paragraph*{Network.} Entity/option tokens are kept without hand-crafted features (option tokens add the embeddings of the cards they act on); a 4-layer pre-norm transformer ($d{=}192$, 2.6M parameters) feeds a policy, a count head for $k$-of-$n$ choices, a value head and a \emph{belief} head predicting which cards are in the opponent's hand, supervised by the opponent's own view stored in the replay. Training uses 453k decisions from the top 3{,}000 replays (split 90/5/5 by game). The value head overfits the 3k game outcomes, so it is fine-tuned on 8k self-play games with the policy anchored on replays.

\paragraph*{Search.} At each decision $K$ determinizations are sampled (own deck/prizes uniform; opponent decklist the most consistent of 120 decklists mined from replays and ladder games; opponent hand uniform or from the belief head) and PUCT is run with network priors and value leaves on the simulator; forced moves are played by the policy and root visits are summed over worlds. A time manager spends $\min(c, (T_\text{left}-r)/q)$ seconds per decision. Expert iteration (ExIt) fine-tunes the network on visit distributions of MCTS self-play.

\paragraph*{Protocol.} Offline metrics on held-out games; online, a fixed gauntlet of six opponents (two first-generation agents and the Gen-2 policy piloting four meta decks) with a simulated time budget, 95\% Wilson intervals, paired head-to-head ablations and an SPRT ($H_0$: 0, $H_1$: +30 Elo).

\paragraph*{Offline and deck.} The Gen-2 policy predicts 70.0\% of expert moves (uniform: 21.7\%); the belief head beats counting even with the true decklist, and self-play makes the value better than a constant (Tab.~\ref{tab:abl}). The deck dominating top replays (Dragapult) scores 0.49 with this policy, Kangaskhan/Crustle 0.73 (240 games each).

\paragraph*{Search.} Search is the main source of strength (Fig.~\ref{fig:scaling}): 0.75 for the raw policy, 0.87 at 256 simulations and 0.90 for the deployed agent, reproduced on a second seed. Belief-sampled hands do not help, and four determinizations lose to one deeper tree ($-75$ Elo): at these depths the exact hand rarely matters. ExIt with 64-simulation targets leaves the raw policy unchanged but strengthens search (+49 Elo); a third round and searching $k$-of-$n$ choices did not help.

\paragraph*{Ladder.} Gen-2 agents rate 737--770 against 500--650 for Gen-1 (Tab.~\ref{tab:gen}), and the one local ranking testable there (1 $>$ 4 worlds) held on the ladder (768 vs 737).

\section{Discussion and conclusions}
Learning alone plateaued: richer models and offline/online RL improved imitation, not play, while a smaller network guiding search on the true engine gained $\approx$120 rating points. Belief modelling helps prediction, not play. Limitations: four of six gauntlet opponents share its network and chose its deck; the gauntlet saturates (0.90) while 800+-rated opponents remain hard (0.36--0.52).

\section{Statement on the use of AI}\label{sec:ai}
I used AI assistants throughout the project, in two phases.

\emph{First generation (\S\ref{sec:gen1}).} I worked with Gemini 3.8 Flash (Google) as a coding assistant. It wrote a large part of the code (replay parsing, feature extraction, the relational transformer, the BC/IQL/PPO training scripts, the light search and the deck tournament) and helped me debug the pipeline and analyse Kaggle replays of lost games.

\emph{Second generation (\S\ref{sec:gen2}) and report.} I then switched to Claude Opus 5.5 (Anthropic), used as an agentic assistant. It reviewed the first-generation system and helped me plan the restart, wrote most of the new code (parser, network, MCTS, evaluation harness, submission builder), launched training and evaluation runs on my machine through a job queue, analysed the logs and ladder episodes, produced the figures and helped draft and edit this report.

I chose the goals and the main design decisions (what to keep, what to drop, which agents to submit), ran the Kaggle submissions, reviewed the code and checked every reported number against the logged outputs in the repository (\texttt{experiments/}, \texttt{RESULTS.md}). I understand and can explain the methods and the code, and I take full responsibility for the content of this report.

\paragraph*{Training and compute.} Supervised training: AdamW (lr $4\cdot10^{-4}$, weight decay 0.05, batch 512, 12 epochs, loss weights policy 1, value 0.25, count 0.2, belief 0.05; moves of the losing player weighted 0.5), $\approx$45 min on one RTX 4070 Ti SUPER.
